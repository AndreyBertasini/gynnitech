In daily growth work, the production database alone does not suffice for effective decision-making. Many teams start by querying the OLTP database, which records real-time transactions, to answer business questions. It seems to work at first, but soon issues like slow queries, fragile joins, and inconsistent metrics arise.

## The thesis

OLTP is like the store's cash register: it records every transaction. OLAP is the room where shelves, margins, cohorts, and seasonality are analyzed to understand business growth. The goal is not to add another tool or dashboard but to build a mechanism that speeds up the transition from signal to decision. A good growth system does not promise certainty but lowers the cost of uncertainty.

## Operational framework

The process unfolds in five clear steps:

1. Operational sources: raw data from production systems.
2. Controlled extraction: cleaning and transforming data for analysis.
3. Analytical model: integrating calendar, campaigns, returns, costs, segments, and customer history.
4. Shared metrics: unique and consistent definitions for everyone.
5. Consumption for dashboards, experiments, and decision agents.

This simplicity is intentional. Complexity arises only when traffic, segments, channels, and automations increase. If the basic flow isn't clear in a few steps, the team is likely automating a process they don't yet understand.

## Practical example

An order is perfect in the operational database, but to understand customer lifetime value (LTV), integrated and historical data in an analytical model is necessary. The real value lies not in the single data point but in linking intervention to learning: if results improve, the team knows what to scale; if not, which hypothesis to correct. This way, the system becomes smarter.

## Metrics to watch

- Query execution time
- Number of duplicated definitions
- Completeness of history
- Data freshness

These metrics should be part of a short scorecard, reviewed regularly with concrete decisions attached. If a metric does not influence any decision, it is just a comfort metric.

## Common mistake to avoid

Confusing real data with decision-ready data. Operational data is true but often not interpretable. This mistake is common because it feels productive: it generates activity, meetings, and charts, but growth engineering's value is measured by the quality of the learning cycle that remains.

## Team checklist

- Which decision needs to become clearer?
- Which event or data source makes behavior observable?
- Which risk do we not want to worsen while optimizing?
- Who can truly change the process after seeing the result?

If any answer is vague, it's better to pause before implementing. Real speed is not starting immediately but avoiding rework due to unclear hypotheses, data, or decision criteria.

## Opinion

In an AI-driven world, weak growth will become even noisier. It will be easy to generate ideas, texts, segments, and automations, but much rarer to build systems that distinguish signal from noise. The growth engineer of the future will not be just a technician but someone who can design tests, boundaries, feedback, and operational memory. Those who can do this do not chase AI; they embed it in a controlled process.

## Next step

For each critical metric, identify which operational tables it comes from and what transformation makes it readable. Bringing this question to the next review shifts the conversation from vague opinions to a system that can learn.
