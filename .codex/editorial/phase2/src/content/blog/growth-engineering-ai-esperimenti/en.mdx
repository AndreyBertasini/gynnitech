In *Growth Engineering*, growth is treated as a systemic effort combining product, data, code, experiments, and operational responsibility. With AI, however, you are not just testing a static screen but a behavior that can change over time and context.

AI products can produce different outputs for the same input, making it harder to understand what the user actually experienced. This fundamentally changes how experimentation and decision-making happen.

## The thesis

Testing a static page is like testing a door: the outcome is predictable and repeatable. Testing an AI agent is like testing a colleague: it matters not only what they do but how they think, when they ask for help, and how they act afterward.

The goal is not to add another tool or dashboard but to build a mechanism that lowers the cost of uncertainty and speeds up the transition from signal to decision. A good growth system doesn't promise certainty but helps manage uncertainty effectively.

In daily work, this means shifting the mindset around code: a change isn't complete when deployed but when it can be observed, compared to a hypothesis, and turned into a decision - to release, iterate, stop, or investigate further.

## Operational framework

1. User input
2. Available context
3. Policies and tools
4. Output
5. Feedback
6. Human or automated evaluation

This simple framework is the foundation. Complexity grows with traffic, segments, channels, and automations, but if the basic flow isn't clear, the team is likely automating a process they don't fully understand yet.

## Practical example

An agent that writes emails should be evaluated on multiple dimensions: draft acceptance, corrections, tone, safety, time saved, and ability to stop appropriately.

The value lies not in individual interventions but in linking interventions to learning. If results improve, the team knows what to scale; if not, they know which assumptions to revise. This way the system becomes smarter.

## Metrics to watch

- Task success
- Human edit distance
- Unsafe output rate
- Appropriate handoff
- Time saved

These metrics must be part of a concise scorecard, reviewed regularly with decisions attached. If a metric doesn't influence choices, it's likely just comforting data.

## Common mistake to avoid

Measuring only whether the user clicked "send." The user might send mediocre content out of fatigue or haste. This mistake is common because it generates activity and charts but doesn't measure the real value of the learning cycle.

## Team checklist

- Which decision needs to become clearer?
- Which event or data source makes behavior observable?
- Which risk must not worsen during optimization?
- Who can truly change the process after seeing the results?

If any answer is vague, it's better to pause before implementing. True speed is not rushing but avoiding rework caused by unclear hypotheses or data.

## Opinion

In an AI-driven world, weak growth will become even noisier. It will be easy to generate ideas, texts, segments, and automations but much harder to build systems that separate signal from noise.

The growth engineer of the future won't be just a technician but a designer of tests, boundaries, feedback, and operational memory. Those who master this don't chase AI - they embed it in controllable processes.

## Next step

For every AI experiment, add at least one output quality metric and one safety metric.

Bringing this question into the next review is already a small act of growth engineering: it shifts the conversation from vague opinions to a system that can learn.
