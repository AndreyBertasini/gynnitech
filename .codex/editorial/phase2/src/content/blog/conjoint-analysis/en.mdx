In 1985, Green and Srinivasan, pioneers of modern conjoint analysis, published a case that transformed product decision-making in consumer goods companies. Procter & Gamble had invested millions in developing an advanced stain-removal detergent formula. Internal focus groups praised it, but the market test failed. Why? Consumers said they were willing to pay more for the formula but chose the cheaper product on the shelf.

Conjoint analysis addresses this paradox: instead of asking consumers what they want, it puts them in realistic choice scenarios with explicit trade-offs. Their decisions reveal true preferences, not just stated ones.

Companies like Apple, Airbnb, and Spotify use this method to optimize products and pricing. It's accessible: with a spreadsheet and Python, you can run a serious study on a reasonable budget. Let's explore how it works and how to apply it for concrete decisions.

## Real-world problem

Traditional market research questions ask how important each feature is but don't force trade-offs. As a result, every attribute is rated "very important," making the answers useless for practical decisions.

In reality, every purchase involves compromises: paying more for a better feature or saving money by accepting less. Conjoint analysis simulates this by showing options with different attribute combinations and asking respondents to choose. Aggregated choices reveal the real importance of each attribute.

## Conceptual model

Imagine choosing a smartphone among three options with different brands, battery life, cameras, and prices. Your choice implicitly communicates which features you value most without needing verbal explanations.

## Rigorous formalization

There are several conjoint analysis types:

- **Choice-Based Conjoint (CBC):** the most common, shows 2-4 profiles per task and asks for the preferred one. It simulates real decisions and enables statistical models like Multinomial Logit.

- **MaxDiff (Best-Worst Scaling):** suited for many attributes, asks respondents to pick the most and least important among a few items per task.

- **Adaptive Conjoint (ACA):** adapts questions based on previous answers, estimating individual preferences.

- **Rating-Based Conjoint:** respondents rate each profile individually; less realistic but simpler.

## Example or case study

For a SaaS project management service, five attributes with three levels each (price, users, integrations, support, storage) are selected. Experimental design chooses an orthogonal subset of profiles to avoid attribute correlations.

A logit model estimates part-worth utilities, representing each level's contribution to perceived utility. For example, low price has high utility, high price low utility.

Relative importance is calculated as the range of utilities per attribute divided by the total range. In this case, price accounts for 68%, storage 15%, and support only 3%.

## Lab / exercise

**Basic level:** Define 4-5 attributes for a product of your choice, with 2-3 credible levels each.

**Intermediate level:** Design a reduced orthogonal design for a CBC study with at least 12 profiles.

**Research-grade:** Simulate CBC data for 300 respondents, estimate a logit model, and interpret part-worth utilities.

**Recommended datasets and materials:** Simulated Python datasets on GinnyTech GitHub, pyDOE2 library for experimental design, scikit-learn for logit models.

## Common mistake to avoid

- Too many attributes in CBC cause cognitive overload and random answers.
- Unrealistic levels distort responses.
- Non-representative samples invalidate results.
- Ignoring segmentation hides different preferences.
- Non-orthogonal experimental designs confuse analysis.

## Conclusion

Conjoint analysis teaches that stated preferences often differ from those revealed by actual choices. It forces consumers to decide in a realistic context, providing valuable quantitative data for product and pricing decisions based on facts, not intuition. Without it, you risk asking "what do you want?" and trusting answers that don't reflect real behavior. The discipline of making decisions under uncertainty gains a powerful, pragmatic tool.
