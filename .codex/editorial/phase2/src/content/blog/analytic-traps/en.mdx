Interpreting data correctly is the real challenge in analytics, not just collecting numbers. Often, technically flawless analysis leads to wrong conclusions because of reasoning errors that overlook the complexity of causality and the inherent limits of data.

## Real-world problem

A telling example comes from Facebook in 2012: an internal study showed users clicking "Like" buttons had higher retention rates. The obvious conclusion was that increasing Likes would improve retention. In reality, the causality was reversed: loyal users clicked Like more often. Optimizing Likes wouldn't have increased retention.

Such errors are common and cost companies millions. The discipline of decision-making under uncertainty requires recognizing and avoiding these traps.

## Conceptual model

Common pitfalls in data analysis include:

- Confusing correlation with causation
- Survivorship bias
- Simpson's paradox
- Sampling bias
- Confirmation bias
- Base rate neglect
- Goodhart's Law
- Extrapolation bias
- Anchoring bias
- Recency bias

Each represents a way our interpretation can stray from reality, leading to poor decisions.

## Rigorous formalization

### 1. Correlation vs Causation

Correlation between two variables does not imply one causes the other. Without experimental design or control groups, causality cannot be claimed.

**How to avoid:** use Randomized Controlled Trials (RCTs) or, if impossible, techniques like Difference-in-Differences to isolate causal effects.

### 2. Survivorship Bias

Analyzing only those who "survive" a process distorts data perception. Examples include bullet holes on returning planes and active customers ignoring churned ones.

**How to avoid:** collect data on those who left or are missing from the main dataset.

### 3. Simpson's Paradox

Aggregated data can hide opposing effects in subgroups. Berkeley admissions data showed apparent gender bias in aggregate, but faculty-level analysis revealed the opposite.

**How to avoid:** always segment data by key dimensions before drawing conclusions.

### 4. Sampling Bias

A non-representative sample leads to wrong conclusions. The 1936 Literary Digest poll is a classic example.

**How to avoid:** assess who is missing from your sample and whether the dataset reflects the real population.

### 5. Confirmation Bias

Seeking only data that confirms your hypothesis and ignoring contradictory evidence.

**How to avoid:** actively look for data that could disprove your conclusion.

### 6. Base Rate Neglect

Ignoring prior probabilities can lead to misinterpretation of results.

**How to avoid:** always compare with historical baselines for your specific segment.

### 7. Goodhart's Law

Optimizing a proxy metric can harm the real business if that metric doesn't reflect the ultimate goal.

**How to avoid:** monitor primary metrics alongside proxy ones.

### 8. Extrapolation Bias

Assuming the past predicts the future without considering context changes.

**How to avoid:** include scenario analysis and avoid linear extrapolations.

### 9. Anchoring Bias

The first number presented anchors perception of subsequent data.

**How to avoid:** always provide context and historical comparisons.

### 10. Recency Bias

Overweighting recent events over a broader view.

**How to avoid:** analyze data over sufficiently long windows to capture natural cycles.

## Example or case study

### Amazon and latency

Amazon demonstrated through controlled experiments that every 100 milliseconds of increased latency costs 1% in sales. This is causal proof, unlike many uncontrolled correlations.

### Simpson's paradox in A/B testing

A test shows version B wins on desktop and mobile but loses overall because traffic is unevenly distributed. Without segmentation, the conclusion would be wrong.

### Sampling bias in satisfaction surveys

Python code simulates how survey respondents are a non-representative subset, overestimating average satisfaction.

## Lab / exercise

**Basic level:** Identify a correlation in a simple dataset and assess if it could be causal.

**Intermediate level:** Segment data to check for Simpson's paradox.

**Research-grade:** Design a controlled experiment (RCT) to test a marketing campaign's effect.

**Recommended datasets and materials:** real marketing campaign data, partial response survey data, segmented A/B test examples.

## Common mistake to avoid

Presenting aggregated results without segmenting data, ignoring control groups, or overlooking missing data are common errors leading to wrong decisions.

## Conclusion

Data analysis demands humility and rigor. No model is perfect, but recognizing inherent limits and biases enables better decisions. The key is always questioning plausible alternatives and missing data before concluding. Only then can uncertainty become a competitive advantage.
