In 2007, sociologist Roland Fryer published a study revealing that job candidates with "African American" names received 50% fewer callbacks than identical candidates with "white" names. What many missed was that the distribution of callbacks differed radically, not just the average. Fryer used boxplots to show that candidates with "African American" names not only had fewer callbacks on average but also a compressed distribution lacking high-quality company callbacks.

This example illustrates why boxplots matter: they do not replace the mean but reveal what the mean hides - the shape of the distribution, concentration of values, and anomalies. Two groups can share the same average but have completely different distributions, and the boxplot makes this clear.

## Anatomy of a Boxplot: Element by Element

Invented by John Tukey in 1970, the boxplot is a tool for exploring data before confirming hypotheses. Consider delivery times for 100 e-commerce orders:

Each element has a precise meaning:

| Element | Statistical Definition | Practical Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| Median | 50th percentile | The true central point: half the orders arrive before this time |
| Q1 | 25th percentile | The fastest 25% of orders arrive by this time |
| Q3 | 75th percentile | 75% of orders arrive by this time |
| IQR | Q3 minus Q1 | Variability of typical values, excluding extremes |
| Lower whisker | Q1 minus 1.5 × IQR | Lower bound of normal range |
| Upper whisker | Q3 plus 1.5 × IQR | Upper bound of normal range |
| Outliers | Values beyond whiskers | Anomalies worthy of attention |

The whisker formula includes 99.3% of values for a normal distribution.

## Reading Each Element: Detailed Insight

### Quartiles

Quartiles split data into four equal parts, with the median representing the center. The median is preferred over the mean when outliers strongly influence the mean.

### Interquartile Range (IQR)

IQR measures variability of the central data, excluding the lowest and highest 25%. A small IQR indicates consistency; a large IQR indicates high variability.

### Whiskers

Whiskers define the "normal" data range per Tukey's formula. Values beyond whiskers are rare and deserve attention.

### Outliers

Outliers represent special cases to investigate: remote deliveries, exceptionally long customer service responses, or very large purchases. They are not noise but opportunities or problems to analyze.

## Why the Boxplot Beats the Mean: Anscombe's Quartet

In 1973, Francis Anscombe showed four datasets with identical summary statistics but completely different distributions, proving that means and other summaries can mislead without visualization.

### Example of Two Departments

Two departments with identical average daily sales (100 euros) can have very different distributions: one stable and predictable, the other with large swings and outliers. The boxplot reveals this difference, crucial for planning.

## Boxplot vs Histogram vs Violin Plot

| Visualization | Best Use | Pros | Cons |
|---|---|---|---|
| Boxplot | Comparing 5-20 distributions | Compact, clearly shows outliers | Hides full distribution shape |
| Histogram | Analyzing 1-2 distributions | Shows detailed shape | Hard to compare many groups |
| Violin Plot | Combines boxplot and histogram | Shows shape and quartiles | Visually complex |
| Density Plot | Very smooth continuous data | Elegant, natural | Less precise on outliers |

Use boxplots for multiple group comparisons, histograms to understand single distributions, and violin plots for a mix of both.

## 5 Patterns to Recognize Immediately

1. Narrow box: concentrated data, stable and predictable process.
2. Wide box: high variability, unpredictable process.
3. Off-center median: asymmetric distribution, mean misleading.
4. Many outliers: cases to investigate, opportunities or problems.
5. Comparative boxplots: show changes in median and variability.

## When to Use Boxplots

| Use When... | Avoid When... |
|---|---|
| Comparing groups | Few data points (<20 per group) |
| Seeking outliers | Showing time trends |
| Analyzing continuous distributions | Data is categorical |
| Suspecting differences hidden by the mean | Audience unfamiliar with statistical charts |

For non-technical audiences, prefer histograms or bar charts; for analytic teams, use boxplots.

## Three Immediate Business Applications

- Customer service response times: identify agents with inconsistent performance and outliers to investigate.
- AOV distribution: segment by channel to understand differences hidden by the mean.
- Sales performance: distinguish stable sellers from volatile ones for targeted interventions.

## The Fundamental Lesson

Data visualization is integral to analysis. The boxplot is the simplest, most powerful tool to reveal the structure behind the mean. Before accepting a mean, always ask to see the boxplot.
