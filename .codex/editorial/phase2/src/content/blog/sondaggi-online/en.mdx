Back in 2011, Airbnb faced a common challenge: their conversion rate was low, but their quantitative data couldn't explain why. They had Google Analytics, heatmaps, funnel data - but no clear answers. So they sent a survey to users who viewed a listing but didn't book, asking one simple question: "What stopped you from booking?"

The answers revealed issues invisible in behavioral data: poor photos, unclear pricing, and too few trustworthy reviews. Airbnb addressed these points and saw significant improvement. This story shows why, even in the big data era, online surveys remain essential: behavioral data tells you what people do, but only their words explain why.

Yet many online surveys suffer from statistical flaws - hidden biases, inadequate sample sizes, ambiguous questions - resulting in data that looks valid but misleads.

## Sampling Fundamentals: Probabilistic vs Non-Probabilistic

To produce generalizable results, understanding sampling is key.

### Probabilistic Sampling

Every individual has a known, non-zero chance of selection. Main methods include:

- **Simple Random Sampling**: equal chance for all. Most rigorous but hard to implement online.
- **Stratified Sampling**: population divided into strata (age, gender), then random samples from each, ensuring representation.
- **Systematic Sampling**: select every Kth unit from an ordered list. Easy to implement and valid if no hidden patterns exist.

These allow generalization with calculable margins of error, e.g., "58% of customers prefer payment method X ±5% margin of error at 95% confidence."

### Non-Probabilistic Sampling

Based on convenience or participant willingness, including:

- **Convenience Sampling**: surveying whoever is available, like emailing current customers. Fast but not representative.
- **Quota Sampling**: reaching fixed numbers per category without randomization.
- **Snowball Sampling**: participants recruit others, useful for hidden communities but highly biased.

Such data describe the sample, not the population. In Italy, most online surveys use this approach, limiting generalizability.

## Calculating Sample Size

The critical question: how many respondents are needed for reliable results?

Standard formula for probabilistic sampling:

n = (Z² × p × (1-p)) / E²

Where:
- Z = critical value for confidence level (e.g., 1.96 for 95%)
- p = estimated proportion (0.5 if unknown for maximum variability)
- E = desired margin of error (e.g., 0.05 for ±5%)

### Example: NPS Survey

For an e-commerce with 50,000 customers, unknown distribution, 95% confidence, and ±5% margin, about 400 responses are needed.

A tighter margin (±3%) requires roughly 1,100 responses.

## Measurement Scales and Question Coding

Scale choice determines analysis possibilities.

- **Nominal**: unordered categories (e.g., gender). Use frequencies and chi-square tests.
- **Ordinal**: ordered categories with unequal intervals (e.g., satisfaction 1-5). Use medians and nonparametric tests like Spearman.
- **Interval**: ordered with equal intervals but arbitrary zero (e.g., NPS 0-10). Means and parametric tests apply.
- **Ratio**: ordered, equal intervals, natural zero (e.g., euros spent). All statistical analyses valid.

Special scales like Semantic Differential and Visual Analog Scale (VAS) capture nuanced perceptions and intensities.

## Five Common Pitfalls to Avoid

1. **Double-barreled questions**: asking two things at once leads to ambiguous answers.
2. **Leading questions**: wording that nudges responses.
3. **Social desirability bias**: respondents answer what's socially acceptable rather than true.
4. **Ambiguous response options**: vague terms like "recently" or "often" without clear definitions.
5. **Too many mandatory or open-ended questions**: increase dropout rates.

## Statistical Analysis: Chi-Square and Spearman Correlation

- **Chi-Square**: tests relationships between categorical variables. p-`value < 0.05` indicates significance.

- **Spearman**: measures correlation between ordinal variables. Values above 0.7 indicate strong correlation.

## Case Study: E-commerce Customer Satisfaction Survey

A fashion e-commerce wants to understand checkout abandonment.

1. Define objective: identify friction points.
2. Population: 12,500 customers who visited checkout in last 3 months.
3. Sample size: about 400 responses for ±5% margin.
4. Survey structure: screening, open-ended, quantitative, demographic questions.
5. Targeted sending to customers who abandoned carts 24-48 hours after event.
6. Analysis using frequency counts, chi-square, and Spearman to prioritize interventions.

## Peculiarities of the Italian Market

- Formal "Lei" in B2B surveys; "tu" in B2C depending on brand.
- Privacy sensitivity: explicitly mentioning GDPR boosts trust.
- Timing: Tuesday and Wednesday mornings best; avoid August.
- Incentives: Amazon vouchers or aggregated reports increase response rates.
- Length: keep surveys under 5-7 minutes to reduce drop-offs.

## Conclusion

Well-designed surveys take time but yield reliable data for informed decisions. Resist rushing to launch without testing and calibration. Remember, surveys bridge observed behavior and underlying motivations. Use them to truly understand your customers and improve your offerings.

Next time you see a survey claiming "73% of customers want feature X," ask: who responded? How was the question phrased? These answers can radically change your product decisions.
