In *Growth Engineering*, growth is treated as a system: product, data, code, experiments, and operational responsibility. This article picks up that perspective without turning it into abstract theory: sample size is an economic choice as much as a statistical one.

Many tests start without knowing what effect size they could realistically detect. When results are uncertain, teams extend tests arbitrarily.

## The thesis

Running a test without an MDE is like fishing with a net of unknown size: you don't know if the fish are absent or if the net is too wide.

The goal isn't to add another marketing tool or product dashboard. It's to build a mechanism that speeds up the transition from signal to decision. A good growth system doesn't promise certainty; it lowers the cost of uncertainty.

This mindset changes how code is written daily. A change isn't complete when it goes live; it's complete when it can be observed, compared to a hypothesis, and turned into a decision: release, iterate, stop, or investigate further.

## Operational framework

1. Baseline
2. Minimum Detectable Effect (MDE)
3. Statistical power
4. Acceptable error level
5. Maximum test duration
6. Business decision

This framework is intentionally simple. Complexity arises with more traffic, segments, channels, and automation. If the basic flow doesn't fit into five or six clear steps, the team is likely automating a process they don't fully understand.

## Practical example

If checkout converts at 4% and you want to detect a 0.1% improvement, you might need much more traffic than the business can afford to wait for.

The key is not the single intervention but the link between intervention and learning. If results improve, the team knows what to scale; if not, they know which assumptions to revise. Either way, the system gets smarter.

## Metrics to watch

- MDE
- Power
- Baseline conversion
- Opportunity cost of the test

These metrics shouldn't be decorative. They must be part of a concise scorecard, reviewed regularly with an associated decision. If a metric doesn't influence any decision, it's likely a comfort metric.

## Common mistake to avoid

Chasing statistical significance on effects too small to impact the business meaningfully.

This mistake is common because it feels productive: it generates activity, meetings, charts, and often enthusiasm. But growth engineering measures value not by the number of tasks done, but by the quality of the learning cycle that remains.

## Team checklist

- Which decision needs to become clearer?
- What event or data source makes behavior observable?
- Which risk must not worsen while optimizing?
- Who can truly change the process after seeing the results?

If any answer is vague, it's better to pause before implementing. True speed is not starting immediately but avoiding rework caused by unclear hypotheses, data, or decision criteria.

## Opinion

In an AI-driven world, weak growth will become even noisier. Generating ideas, texts, segments, and automations will be easy, but building systems that separate signal from noise will be rare.

That's why the next-cycle growth engineer won't be just a technician but someone who designs tests, boundaries, feedback, and operational memory. Those who master this don't chase AI; they embed it in a controllable process.

## Next step

Before any statistical calculation, ask: what minimum improvement justifies implementation, maintenance, and risk?

Bringing this question into the next review is already a growth engineering act: it shifts the conversation from vague opinions to a system that can learn.
