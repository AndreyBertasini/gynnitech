Growth engineering approaches growth as a systemic effort involving product, data, code, experiments, and operational responsibility. Among its tools, the fake door test stands out as a way to validate real user interest before investing in building a feature. Yet, this method must be applied transparently to preserve user trust.

## The thesis

A fake door test is like a "coming soon" sign: useful if honest, annoying if misleading. The goal is not to add another marketing tool or product dashboard but to create a mechanism that speeds the transition from signal to decision. A good growth system does not promise certainty but lowers the cost of uncertainty.

This mindset changes how code is written daily: a change is not complete when deployed, but when it can be observed, tested against a hypothesis, and turned into an operational choice - whether to release, iterate, stop, or investigate further.

## Operational framework

1. clear promise
2. measurable click or request
3. transparent message
4. alternative option
5. follow-up with interested users

This simple framework is the starting point. Complexity grows with traffic, segments, channels, and automation. If the basic flow can't fit into a few clear steps, the team is likely automating a process it hasn't fully understood yet.

## Practical example

Imagine showing a button labeled "Automate this report." When clicked, it explains the feature is under evaluation and offers a waitlist or a real workaround.

The key is not the single intervention but the link between intervention and learning. If results improve, the team knows what to scale; if not, they know which assumptions to correct. Either way, the system becomes smarter.

## Metrics to watch

- click intent
- conversion to waitlist
- qualitative feedback
- complaints or frustration signals

These metrics must be part of a concise scorecard, reviewed regularly with associated decisions. If a metric doesn't influence any choice, it's likely a comfort metric.

## Common mistake to avoid

Hiding that the feature doesn't exist. Data gathered under distrust costs more than it's worth.

This mistake is common because it seems productive: it generates activities, meetings, charts, and often enthusiasm. But growth engineering's value is measured by the quality of the learning cycle left behind, not by the number of tasks completed.

## Team checklist

- Which decision needs to become clearer?
- What event or data source makes behavior observable?
- What risk must we avoid worsening while optimizing?
- Who can truly change the process after seeing the result?

If any answer is vague, it's better to pause before implementing. Real speed is not rushing but avoiding rework due to unclear hypotheses, data, or decision criteria.

## Opinion

In an AI-driven world, weak growth will become even noisier. Generating ideas, texts, segments, and automations will be easy; building systems that distinguish signal from noise will be much rarer.

Therefore, the growth engineer of the next cycle won't be just a technician but a designer of tests, limits, feedback, and operational memory. Those who master this won't chase AI - they'll embed it within a controllable process.

## Next step

Write the post-click message before designing the button. If you hesitate to show it, the test is flawed.

Bringing this question to the next review is already a small act of growth engineering: it shifts the conversation from vague opinions to a system that can learn.
