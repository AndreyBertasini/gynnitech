In 2000, Cisco had what executives called "the best-in-class" sales forecasting system. It combined historical data, regression models, and manual input from sales managers. Yet, when the dot-com bubble burst in March 2001, Cisco had to write down 2.2 billion dollars of inventory in a single quarter. The forecast failed to capture the market regime shift.

The issue was not technical but conceptual: the model was built on data from an expansion cycle and had never seen a contraction cycle. It extrapolated the past into a structurally different future. This story teaches that forecasting is not prophecy but an estimation with explicit error margins and stated assumptions, improving decisions beyond mere guesswork.

## Why Forecasting Matters: The Science of Decision-Making

Answering "How much will we earn next quarter?" with a number grounded in multiple statistical methods lets you plan inventory, hiring, and marketing budgets. Answering "Who knows?" means making decisions blindly.

Amazon Web Services uses demand forecasts to decide how much server capacity to add each quarter. A 10% overestimate wastes capital expenditure; a 10% underestimate loses customers. Even a 2% accuracy improvement translates to hundreds of millions.

The same principle applies regardless of scale: better forecasting is a learnable skill.

## Approach 1: Moving Average  -  Simplicity and Robustness

The moving average calculates the average sales over the last N periods to predict the next. With over a century of use, it remains popular for its robustness and interpretability.

### How It Works

Forecast for period t+1 = Average sales over the last N periods.

For example, with monthly sales of 100,000, 120,000, 110,000, 130,000, 125,000, 135,000 euros over six months, the 3-month moving average is (130,000 + 125,000 + 135,000) / 3 = 130,000 euros.

Choosing N affects responsiveness and stability:
- Small N (2-3): quick to react but sensitive to outliers
- Large N (6-12): stable but slow to detect structural changes

### When to Use It

| Pros | Cons |
|---|---|
| Easy to compute and interpret | Does not capture trend or seasonality |
| Quick to implement in Excel | Slow to react to structural changes |
| Robust to data errors | Equal weighting of all observations |

Suitable for stable businesses with steady demand and little seasonality.

## Approach 2: Linear Regression  -  Capturing Trend

If sales show a clear upward or downward trend, linear regression fits a line describing this trend and projects it forward.

### How It Works

Sales = a + b × Time

Where a is the baseline and b is average growth per period. For example, b = 5,000 means sales grow by 5,000 euros monthly on average.

### Enhancing with Explanatory Variables

Adding variables like marketing budget, temperature, or working days improves explanatory power. Walmart, for instance, incorporates weather forecasts to anticipate seasonal sales.

### When to Use It

| Pros | Cons |
|---|---|
| Captures growth or decline trends | Assumes linearity often unrealistic |
| Easy to explain and interpret | Does not handle seasonality |
| Can include causal variables | Sensitive to outliers |

Ideal for businesses with clear trends and little seasonality or as a baseline.

## Approach 3: ARIMA and SARIMA  -  The Industry Standard

For businesses with trend and seasonality, ARIMA is the go-to model. It combines autoregressive terms, differencing for stationarity, and moving average of errors. SARIMA adds seasonality.

### How It Works

- AR: dependence on past sales
- I: differencing to remove trend
- MA: correction based on past errors

### When to Use It

| Pros | Cons |
|---|---|
| Handles trend and seasonality | Requires long historical data (24+ months) |
| Provides confidence intervals | More complex to configure |
| Interpretable and well-established | Assumes future resembles past |

Suitable for businesses with sufficient history and seasonal patterns.

## Measuring Accuracy: MAPE and MAE

To compare models, use metrics like MAE (mean absolute error) and MAPE (mean absolute percentage error). A MAPE of 5-10% is excellent, 10-20% acceptable, above 20% unreliable.

## The Conceptual Pitfall: Precision vs Accuracy

Forecasts are estimates with confidence intervals, not exact numbers. False precision without uncertainty is more dangerous than explicit uncertainty. NASA, for example, uses "cones of uncertainty" in spacecraft trajectories.

## Conclusion: Iteration Leads to Improvement

Perfectly predicting the future is impossible. Predicting well enough to make better decisions is achievable and measurable. Start with moving averages, move to regression if there's a trend, scale to SARIMA with more mature data. The goal is not complexity but suitability and honesty about uncertainty.

Forecasting is the discipline of making decisions under uncertainty, balancing simplicity, interpretability, and accuracy.
