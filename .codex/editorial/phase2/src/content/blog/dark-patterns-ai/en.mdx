Have you ever faced digital products where canceling a subscription feels like an obstacle course, rejecting an offer is hidden behind multiple clicks, and saying no always seems like the wrong choice? Now imagine an AI agent tasked with "improving conversion" without ethical or transparency limits.

The risk is clear: AI can generate persuasive variants, microcopy, flows, and notifications faster than any human team. If the sole goal is to improve a metric, dark patterns can become an automated industry.

## What is a dark pattern

A dark pattern is a design choice that nudges users toward actions that are not fully free, clear, or desired. Common examples include:

- making it hard to cancel a service;
- hiding additional costs;
- creating false urgency;
- confusing privacy options;
- pre-selecting invasive choices;
- making optional things seem mandatory.

An AI agent driven only by conversion metrics may suggest these strategies because they appear effective. It's up to the human team to recognize the limits and say no.

## The problem with the brief

If you ask an AI agent to "increase conversions," it will optimize just that. But if the brief includes "increase conversions while maintaining clarity, choice, and trust," everything changes.

An effective brief should include:

1. the business objective;
2. user respect principles;
3. forbidden actions;
4. guardrail metrics to prevent ethical slippage;
5. cases requiring human review.

Ethics cannot depend on the mood of whoever reads the output. It must be embedded in the system.

## How to spot a risky proposal

When the agent suggests a test, ask yourself:

- will the user understand what is happening?
- is saying no easy?
- are important details clearly visible?
- is the pressure proportionate?
- is the benefit to the user real?
- would we want to publicly explain this choice?

This last question is crucial: if a tactic works only when hidden, it is probably not a good tactic.

## Growth that does not burn relationships

A dark pattern can win a short test, boosting clicks, sign-ups, or renewals. But it often leaves behind frustration, distrust, and negative word of mouth.

AI agents should help create clearer products, not ones better at confusing.

True optimization is not convincing someone to do something they did not want. It's removing obstacles when that thing brings real value.

## Applying these principles without complicating work

To make **Dark Patterns and AI** practical, don't start with the newest tool. Start where the team wastes time, debates without data, or makes decisions with incomplete information. That's where you see if the topic has operational value or is just a nice slide idea.

The rule is simple: an AI agent should not be treated like a clever chat. It needs clear inputs, limited tools, controlled memory, and an explicit rule to hand off decisions to a person when risk rises.

A useful sequence is:

1. define which data the agent can access and which are off-limits;
2. write the expected outcome in verifiable terms, not vague intentions;
3. decide when human review is needed before sending or saving output;
4. measure time saved, errors avoided, and cases where the agent stops.

## What to measure to know if it works

The right question is not "did we use AI?" or "did we add a new dashboard?" The right question is: **which decision became faster, clearer, or safer?**

Measure at least three levels: operational time saved, quality of the result, and team trust in the process. Time alone can mislead: a faster flow that's less controllable is not an improvement. Quality alone can mislead: a perfect system that's too slow won't enter daily work.

A very concrete final check: ask those using the process to explain what they would do tomorrow with this information. If the answer is vague, it's not a technology problem: there's still a missing clear connection between data, responsibility, and action.
