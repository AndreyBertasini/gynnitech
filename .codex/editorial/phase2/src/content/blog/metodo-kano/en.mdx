In 1984, Japanese professor Noriaki Kano took an unusual step for an academic: instead of staying in the lab building theories, he went straight to talk to customers. This choice transformed how companies approach product development.

His key insight was that not all product features generate the same satisfaction. Some, if missing, cause strong dissatisfaction; others, if present, leave customers indifferent; and some, called "attractive," surprise customers with unexpected delight.

Companies like Toyota, Apple, and Booking.com have used Kano's model to guide their feature decisions. It helps focus budgets and efforts on what truly matters.

## Real-world problem

How do you decide which product features to develop when resources are limited and customer expectations vary? How do you avoid investing in features that don't improve satisfaction or even harm the experience?

## Conceptual model

Kano identified five categories of product attributes:

1. **Must-Be:** features customers take for granted. Their absence causes dissatisfaction; their presence is expected.
2. **Performance:** the more of these features, the higher the satisfaction.
3. **Attractive:** unexpected features that delight customers when present but don't cause dissatisfaction if absent.
4. **Indifferent:** features that don't affect satisfaction.
5. **Reverse:** features some customers love and others hate.

## Rigorous formalization

To classify a feature, Kano suggests a paired-question survey:

- How would you feel if the product had this feature?
- How would you feel if the product did not have this feature?

Responses combined via a matrix determine the feature's category. For example, positive to presence and negative to absence indicates Must-Be; enthusiastic to presence and indifferent to absence indicates Attractive.

Classification matrix:

| Functional \ Dysfunctional | Like | Expect | Indifferent | Can tolerate | Dislike |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Like | Questionable | Attractive | Attractive | Attractive | Performance |
| Expect | Reverse | Indifferent | Indifferent | Indifferent | Must-Be |
| Indifferent | Reverse | Indifferent | Indifferent | Indifferent | Must-Be |
| Can tolerate | Reverse | Indifferent | Indifferent | Indifferent | Must-Be |
| Dislike | Reverse | Reverse | Reverse | Reverse | Questionable |

## Example or case study

An Italian natural cosmetics e-commerce applied Kano's method with a survey of 300 customers. Some planned features were Indifferent to most, while an unplanned feature - personalized free samples - was highly Attractive.

This led to reallocating budget away from useless investments toward features that generate real value and loyalty, achieving over 600% ROI in 18 months.

Intermediate level: Build a Kano questionnaire with functional and dysfunctional questions for at least 10 features and collect 100+ responses.

Research-grade: Analyze data calculating satisfaction and dissatisfaction coefficients per feature and integrate results with prioritization frameworks like RICE.

## Common mistake to avoid

Many small and medium Italian companies spend most of their budget on Performance features, neglect Must-Be features already satisfied, and ignore Attractive ones. The result is technically sound products lacking differentiation and word of mouth.

## Quiz or checkpoint

- Which feature category causes dissatisfaction if missing but no satisfaction if present?
- How are features that pleasantly surprise customers classified?
- Why is it important to consider Reverse features in your roadmap?

## Conclusion

Kano's method teaches that the real challenge is understanding what customers find important - not what we think. Must-Be features are invisible in surveys because they're expected; Attractive features are often unknown even to customers. Only by digging deeper and applying a rigorous method can you make effective development decisions under uncertainty.

In a competitive market, adding features isn't enough - you must choose the right ones that create genuine "wow" moments and loyalty. Kano's model offers a concrete discipline for this challenge.
