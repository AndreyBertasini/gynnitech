In 2012, the Obama For America team faced a critical decision: which subject line to use for an email sent to 4 million subscribers to raise campaign funds. Instead of relying on intuition, they ran 18 A/B tests on the subject line. The winner, "I will be outspent," generated 2.67 million dollars more than the baseline. This is a clear example of how rigorous testing can translate into millions earned.

Yet many smaller companies run tests with too small samples, getting seemingly significant results (p-`value < 0.05`) in just a few days, only to find no real benefit later. The difference is not tools or budget but a deep understanding of the statistical limitations of A/B testing.

## Mistake 1: Too Small Sample Size

Ron Kohavi, former head of Microsoft's experimentation team, estimates only one-third of A/B tests in large companies produce reliable results, dropping below 10% in smaller companies. The main cause is insufficient sample size, making results indistinguishable from chance due to natural variability in human behavior.

For example, a difference between 10 conversions out of 100 visitors (10%) and 12 out of 100 (12%) may look like a 20% improvement but is likely just noise with such a small sample.

### Calculating Required Sample Size

To detect a 5% lift on a 3% baseline conversion rate, you need about 52,000 visitors per variant with 80% power and 95% significance. This means a site with 1,000 visitors per day must run the test for 52 days to get reliable results.

| Baseline CR | Relative Lift | Sample Size per Variant | Days (1,000 vis/day) |
|-------------|---------------|-------------------------|----------------------|
| 1%          | 10%           | ~150,000                | 150                  |
| 3%          | 10%           | ~52,000                 | 52                   |
| 5%          | 10%           | ~30,000                 | 30                   |

### Python for Sample Size Calculation

For precise calculation, use a z-test for proportions:

- Define baseline conversion rate and minimum detectable lift.
- Set significance level (α) and power (1-β).
- Calculate sample size per variant.

If traffic is limited, focus on tests expecting larger lifts; otherwise, tests may last months without useful results.

## Mistake 2: Peeking at Data Early

Checking test results multiple times before the planned end drastically increases false positive rates from the theoretical 5% to nearly 40%. Each early look effectively performs multiple tests without adjusting significance thresholds.

### How to Avoid Peeking

- Decide test duration upfront and stick to it.
- For high-traffic platforms, consider sequential testing methods with always-valid p-values, allowing early stopping only when evidence is overwhelming.

## Mistake 3: Multiple Concurrent Tests

Testing multiple variants simultaneously without adjusting significance levels increases false positive risk. For example, with 5 variants, the chance of at least one false positive is about 18.5%.

### Statistical Corrections

- Bonferroni correction divides α by the number of comparisons but is very conservative.
- Benjamini-Hochberg controls the false discovery rate (FDR), less conservative and better for many simultaneous tests.

## Mistake 4: Ignoring Seasonality and Weekly Cycles

Many businesses behave differently on weekdays versus weekends. Running a test only on weekdays can lead to wrong conclusions by optimizing for one user segment while ignoring another.

### Example

A clothing e-commerce sees double conversion rates on weekends compared to weekdays, with different buying behaviors. A variant adding urgency may hurt conversions on weekdays but boost them on weekends.

### Rule

Run tests for at least two full weekly cycles (2-4 weeks) to capture these variations.

## Mistake 5: Optimizing Proxy Metrics Instead of Real Outcomes

Optimizing surrogate metrics like button click rates may not translate into increased revenue or conversions. Choose primary metrics that reflect real economic impact.

| Level       | Metric                      | Use                                  |
|-------------|-----------------------------|-------------------------------------|
| Primary     | Revenue, Conversions         | Direct business impact measurement  |
| Secondary   | Add-to-cart, Signups         | Reasonable proxies                   |
| Guardrail   | Bounce rate, Error rate      | Monitoring to avoid harm             |
| Surrogate   | CTR, Scroll depth            | Hypothesis generation, not decisions|

## Mistake 6: Skipping A/A Tests

Before running A/B tests, an A/A test (two identical versions) verifies the testing system has no bias or randomization issues. A significant p-value in an A/A test signals problems to fix before proceeding.

## Mistake 7: Confusing Statistical Significance with Practical Importance

A statistically significant result may have negligible economic impact. Defining the Minimum Detectable Effect (MDE) in economic terms before testing helps avoid investing in tests with too low returns.

## Mistake 8: Novelty Effect

New variants may show temporary improvement due to user interest in novelty, which fades over time. Segment analysis by new versus returning users and wait at least 3-4 weeks to distinguish real effects from novelty.

## Mistake 9: Ignoring Guardrail Metrics

Monitoring metrics like error rate, bounce rate, and page load times during tests is essential to avoid regressions that harm user experience or site performance.

## Pre-Test Checklist

Before launching a test, answer these in writing:

1. What is the clear hypothesis?
2. What is the primary metric?
3. What is the economically relevant MDE?
4. What is the sample size and test duration?
5. How will data be segmented?
6. What guardrail metrics will be monitored?
7. What decision criteria (p-value) will be used?

Never change significance thresholds after seeing data.

## Prioritizing Tests with ICE Score

Evaluate each test by Impact, Confidence, and Ease, and prioritize those with the highest scores to optimize resources and outcomes.

## Conclusion

The discipline of decision-making under uncertainty demands patience, rigor, and statistical understanding. Obama's tests succeeded because they respected these principles: adequate samples, no peeking, correct metrics, and thorough documentation. Without this discipline, tests risk providing false confidence and steering business decisions astray.

Investing time in proper test design and analysis prevents months of wasted optimization and enables truly data-driven decisions.
