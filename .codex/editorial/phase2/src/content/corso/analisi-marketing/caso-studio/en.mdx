# Case Study: Data-Driven Marketing Strategy

A SaaS company faces a strategic choice: increase paid budget, clean up nurturing, or shift focus to brand for the next quarter. The data available is real but incomplete: costs, revenue, conversions, saturation, and lead quality tell conflicting stories. This case study shows how to manage these contradictions in a concrete decision-making context.

## Real Problem

In marketing analytics, the real challenge is turning budget, channels, creativity, and audience into measurable decisions without confusing volume, attribution, and incrementality. It's not about appreciating an abstract concept but understanding which decision improves by applying reliable data and awareness of the margin of error.

## Conceptual Model

```mermaid

    A["Business Question"]

    C["Reliable Data"]
    D["Incremental Analysis"]
    E["Budget Decision"]
    A --> B
    B --> C
    C --> D
    D --> E
```

The process is sequential: first define the question, then translate it into an observable hypothesis, verify data quality, and finally decide. Skipping steps leads to elegant but fragile analyses.

| Step | Guiding Question | Expected Output |
|---|---|---|
| Framing | What decision needs to change? | A concrete choice, not curiosity |
| Measurement | Which signal represents the phenomenon? | Metric, source, and granularity |
| Comparison | Against which baseline do I interpret the result? | Benchmark or plausible counterfactual |
| Action | What do I do if the signal crosses the threshold? | Decision, owner, and next check |

## Rigorous Formalization

We formalize the case as a relationship among analysis unit, signal, baseline, and decision:

| Element | Operational Definition |
|---|---|
| Unit | campaign, cohort, touchpoint, or customer segment |
| Signal | incremental margin, CAC payback, corrected conversion rate, lift, or retention generated |
| Baseline | previous period, holdout group, comparable market, or historical benchmark |
| Decision | reallocate resources, change message, stop tactic, or scale experiment |
| Risk | confusing correlation, data quality, and causal decision-making |

A metric is useful only if it reduces uncertainty about a specific decision. If it doesn't change a choice, it's documentation; if it changes a choice without controls, it's risk.

## Example or Case Study

A growth team must decide whether to increase budget on a channel with low CPA but weak marginal sales. We separate useful signal from noise by linking metric, mental model, and economic decision.

| Evidence | Cautious Interpretation | Resulting Decision |
|---|---|---|
| Positive but isolated signal | Phenomenon exists, cause uncertain | Seek baseline or holdout |
| Segment with different response | Average effect hides heterogeneity | Analyze cohorts or subgroups |
| Rising operational cost | Evaluate result on margin | Apply economic thresholds |

## Lab / Exercise

### Basic Level

Write in five lines a real decision linked to the case: objective, primary metric, baseline, main risk, and planned action. Use only one primary metric.

### Intermediate Level

Build a table with at least three segments or scenarios. For each, indicate signal, possible alternative explanation, and control needed before deciding.

### Research-Grade Level

Design a validation plan: hypothesis, required data, exclusion criteria, decision threshold, and post-decision control. Specify what would change your mind.

### Recommended Datasets and Materials

Use campaign exports, media costs, web or app events, CRM, transactions, brand surveys, and consent logs. If no real data, create a synthetic dataset with 200-500 rows including a time column, a segment column, an outcome metric, and an exposure variable.

## Common Mistakes and How to Avoid Them

**Confusing correlation with causation:** two metrics moving together do not imply causation. Only A/B tests or counterfactual analyses establish causality. Always present disclaimers.

**Ignoring seasonality:** comparing November with December without adjusting for holidays leads to misleading insights. Use year-over-year comparisons or deseasonalized moving averages.

**Not validating query grain:** errors like joins duplicating rows or filters applied too late cause wrong results. Always verify row counts at each step.

## Quiz or Checkpoint

1. What concrete decision should this lesson improve?
2. Which baseline makes the result interpretable?
3. Which assumption, if wrong, would change the conclusion?
4. What minimum control would you use before presenting the recommendation?

## Operational Summary

This case study is a useful skill when linking concept, data, and decision. Study it starting from a real problem, formalize the signal, find a credible baseline, build an example, and close with a practical check. It is an advanced lab, estimated study time 28 minutes.
