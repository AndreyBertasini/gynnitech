# Cheat sheet: agentic AI for data work

This cheat sheet tackles a practical challenge: using agentic AI applied to data without turning analytical work into a series of unverifiable answers. The goal is not to ask the model to "do analysis," but to design a workflow where questions, data, checks, and decisions remain visible and traceable.

Building on the previous lesson, it adds a more specific operational layer as in the `case-study-agentic-system-for-analytics-operations`. The professional promise is straightforward: speed up and broaden data work while retaining ownership over definitions, thresholds, segments, and recommendations.

## Real problem

Imagine a weekly review where marketing, product, and data teams must decide what to fix first: funnel, tracking, campaign, model, or pipeline. Without agentic AI, the analyst risks losing hours on repetitive steps; with poorly used agentic AI, they risk delivering a convincing but fragile explanation.

The operational problem arises when a team wants to speed up data work but cannot distinguish between delegable tasks and non-delegable responsibilities. AI can help generate hypotheses, queries, documentation, tests, candidate features, or summaries. However, decisions remain human when they impact budget, roadmap, customers, compliance, access, or production models.

The operational goal is to produce an artifact another professional can review. If in the end you cannot specify which decision changes, which data you observe, and which error you want to avoid, the lesson has not yet become applied competence.

## Conceptual model

The model consists of four levels: task, context, control, and decision. The task describes what AI can do. The context specifies data, definitions, tools, and constraints. The control verifies that the output is not just plausible. The decision clarifies what changes in the team's work.

| Level     | What to clarify                         | Example in data work                        |
|-----------|---------------------------------------|--------------------------------------------|
| Task      | Which part of the work is accelerated? | Query draft, data profile, AutoML checklist, memo |
| Context   | What information makes the output useful? | Schema, metric, period, segment, policy    |
| Control   | How do you detect if the output is wrong? | Grain test, baseline, leakage, owner review |
| Decision  | What action follows if the control holds? | Pipeline fix, experiment, deploy, recommendation |

This model avoids two opposite errors: using AI just as a text autocomplete or giving it autonomy over decisions requiring business judgment. Professional skill lies in drawing the boundary.

## Rigorous formalization

To make the cheat sheet analyzable, first define the unit of work: checklist, policy, gate, tool, eval, trace, or escalation rule. Then link this unit to observable signals: guardrail completeness, owner clarity, expected cost, auditability, and fallback. Finally, declare the expected decision: use a checklist to approve, limit, or stop agentic workflows on data.

| Element          | Required specification                                                                |
|------------------|---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------|
| Unit of analysis | checklist, policy, gate, tool, eval, trace, or escalation rule                         |
| Main signal      | guardrail completeness, owner clarity, expected cost, auditability, and fallback      |
| Baseline         | manual process, previous period, comparable group, or current model                   |
| Decision         | use a checklist to approve, limit, or stop agentic workflows on data                  |
| Guardrail        | privacy, cost, data quality, interpretability, ownership, and operational risk        |
| Main risk        | reducing agentic governance to a checklist without testing real failure modes         |

Formalization is solid when a reviewer can reproduce the logic, critique assumptions, and reach the same decision from the same data. If the result depends on an untracked conversation, the workflow is not mature.

## Operational framework

Use this five-step framework. First, define the decision to improve. Then describe available data, known limits, and business context. Third, ask AI to propose hypotheses or steps, not final conclusions. Fourth, turn proposals into executable checks. Fifth, deliver output separating what we know, what we don't, and what we will do.

| Step         | Guiding question                        | Minimum output                          |
|--------------|---------------------------------------|---------------------------------------|
| Decision     | Which choice should improve?           | Action verb and owner                  |
| Context      | What do we know about the data?        | Sources, grain, period, limits        |
| AI support   | Which part can be accelerated?          | Prompt, tool, or checklist             |
| Verification | Which error could change the conclusion? | Check, guardrail, baseline             |
| Handoff      | Who decides and by what criteria?      | Memo, ticket, model, or dashboard     |

This schema applies to exploratory analysis, SQL, data engineering, AutoML, and agentic workflows. Tools may vary, but the principle remains: no output should be treated as evidence until linked to source, check, and decision.

## Example or case study

Before enabling an agent on query warehouse, the team checks tool scope, allowed data, budget, eval set, logs, approvals, and rollback plan.

The case shows AI is not the center of the work. The center is the decision cycle: question, data, check, recommendation, monitoring. AI helps reduce friction between these steps, but each remains controllable.

| Observed evidence              | Cautious interpretation                      | Recommended action                                   |
|------------------------------|----------------------------------------------|-----------------------------------------------------|
| Workflow produces output faster | Speed is useful only if it does not reduce quality or control | Compare with manual baseline and independent review |
| Model proposes plausible explanations | Linguistic plausibility is not empirical proof | Translate each explanation into a data or process check |
| Result improves a metric       | Could be composition effect, leakage, or seasonality | Segment, verify guardrails, and monitor over time    |

A good case study does not end with "AI worked." It ends with a defensible decision: what to automate, what to keep manual, which metric to monitor, and which condition would stop the process.

## Lab / exercise

### Basic level

Write a one-page sheet for the cheat sheet: decision to support, required inputs, expected output, minimum check, and main risk. Use a simple case, e.g., a conversion variation, a delayed pipeline, or a forecast model to evaluate.

### Intermediate level

Build a table with three scenarios: manual workflow, AI-assisted workflow, and workflow with stronger automation. For each, indicate estimated time, expected quality, operational risk, necessary control, and final owner.

### Research-grade level

Prepare a reviewable decision memo. Include hypotheses, required data, exclusion criteria, quality checks, decision threshold, residual risk, and monitoring plan after the decision. Add a section "what would change the team's mind."

### Recommended datasets and materials

Use agentic checklists, tool catalogs, risk registers, eval sets, policy approvals, and rollout templates. If you lack real data, create a synthetic dataset with at least 200 rows, a time dimension, a segment dimension, a primary metric, and a field representing a possible quality error.

## Common mistakes to avoid

The first mistake is treating the cheat sheet as a label rather than a process. This happens when the team shows generated output without explaining data, checks, and decision. The second mistake is confusing individual productivity with organizational reliability. The third is not deciding upfront which actions are allowed, which require approval, and which are forbidden.

A practical fix is to write three lines before starting:

| Line       | Content                                      |
|------------|----------------------------------------------|
| Decision   | Which concrete choice do we want to improve? |
| Evidence   | Which data or check can support the choice?  |
| Stop       | Which risk would make us stop or ask for review? |

If any of these lines is missing, the workflow is not ready. AI can still be useful as exploratory support but not as a stable part of decision-making.

## Quiz or checkpoint

1. Which concrete decision should this lesson improve?
2. Which part of the workflow can be delegated to AI and which must remain under human ownership?
3. What baseline would you use to measure if the new process truly improves?
4. What failure mode could make an apparently correct output dangerous?
5. What artifact would you deliver to a stakeholder: memo, dashboard, query review, model card, runbook, or checklist?

If you cannot answer specifically, return to the real problem before continuing. A generic answer often signals premature automation.

## Operational summary

The cheat sheet becomes useful when it produces a clearer decision, not when it adds terminology. Use the cycle decision, context, AI support, verification, and handoff to turn the lesson into verifiable practice.

The final rule is simple: the more the workflow uses tools, state, approvals, and trace, the more it must be observable. An output you cannot reconstruct, review, or stop is not professional automation; it is just uncontrolled speed.
