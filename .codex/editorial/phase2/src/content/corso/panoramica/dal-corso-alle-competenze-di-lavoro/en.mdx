# From Course to Work Competencies

A certificate might open a door, but it won't sustain a technical conversation. When asked why a metric changed, which segment to check, or which hypothesis to test, you need to show how you think - not just that you completed a course.

## Starting Point

Bridging the gap between study and work isn't about more theory, but about building evidence: reproducible analyses, mini-projects, decision memos, annotated dashboards, explained and corrected mistakes.

This lesson helps you turn the GinnyTech path into observable skills. Not "I know SQL," but "I diagnosed a retention drop, isolated the segment, wrote the query, explained the risk, and proposed next steps."

## Real-World Problem

The difference between knowledge and competence defines a data career trajectory. Knowing the syntax of an SQL function like `LAG()` is knowledge. Using `LAG()` inside a Common Table Expression (CTE) to calculate month-over-month growth of Monthly Recurring Revenue (MRR) across customer segments and presenting an analysis of a recent price change's impact is competence. The first can be learned in an afternoon; the second requires practice, failure, and deep business context.

Tech-driven companies hunger for impact. They don't want walking SQL dictionaries; they want thinking partners who can translate ambiguous business problems ("why is retention dropping?") into testable hypotheses, analysis plans, flawless technical execution, and strategic recommendations.

This transition requires a mindset shift: the goal is no longer passing exams but building artifacts that solve problems. Your portfolio is that artifact - not a collection of queries or messy notebooks, but a complete product with a clear objective, methodology, quantitative results, and conclusions.

Think of it as your personal R&D lab. Each project demonstrates your ability to navigate the full analysis lifecycle: from cleaning and modeling raw data to extracting signal from noise and effectively communicating complex results to non-technical audiences. Knowledge is passive; competence is active. Knowledge helps you pass the initial CV screening; competence gets you through multiple technical interviews and the job offer.

## The Portfolio as a Real-World Thesis

An effective data analytics portfolio is not a gallery of colorful dashboards but a series of case studies showing your analytical thought process. Each project should be structured like a mini-thesis with a README.md on GitHub serving as an abstract and full report.

An ideal README structure includes:

1. **Context and Business Problem:** What question are you trying to answer? Example: "A music streaming company wants to identify which user behaviors during the first trial week best predict conversion to paid subscription."
2. **Data Used:** Source, granularity (e.g., one event per row, one user per row), and limitations. Transparency is key.
3. **Methodology and Tech Stack:** Explain your choices, not just list tools. Example: "I used dbt to model raw data into fact (`fct_user_sessions`) and dimension (`dim_users`) tables, ensuring quality tests and modularity. Exploratory analysis was done in Python with Pandas and Seaborn, while final funnel analysis was written in SQL for efficiency."
4. **Analysis and Key Results:** Present main charts and summary tables, each with explanatory titles and captions. Example: "Chart 1: Correlation between playlists created in the first week and conversion rate. Users who created at least one playlist had a 35% higher conversion probability."
5. **Conclusions and Recommendations:** What should the company do based on your analysis? Recommendations must be concrete and data-driven. Example: "Recommend modifying onboarding to actively encourage playlist creation within the first three days via targeted emails or in-app prompts."

**Case Study: Spotify's Personalization Approach**
Spotify built its empire by turning listening data into personalized experiences like "Discover Weekly." An analyst could replicate this by using a public music listening dataset (e.g., The Echo Nest Taste Profile Subset) to simulate a product analysis. The goal: "Identify factors that turn a user into a 'power user.'" Metrics might include weekly listening sessions, unique artists listened to, and skip rate. Analysis might reveal users who save at least five songs in their personal library within the first two weeks have a 25 percentage point higher six-month retention. This is no longer a simple query; it's a business argument grounded in data, the kind of work that justifies a six-figure salary.

## Engineering Competence: Tools and Methodologies

Mastering tools means understanding their underlying philosophy and orchestrating them to solve complex problems. The modern data analyst is an information engineer.

The tech stack expected from junior to mid-level candidates revolves around three pillars:

1. **SQL for extraction and manipulation:** Beyond `SELECT`, `FROM`, `WHERE`, fluency with CTEs for code readability, window functions for complex calculations over data partitions, and query optimization on terabytes of data is required.
2. **dbt (Data Build Tool) for transformation and modeling:** dbt revolutionized analytics engineering by bringing software engineering principles (modularity, version control with Git, testing, documentation) to data analysis. Building a Directed Acyclic Graph (DAG) of dbt models, writing data quality tests (e.g., `not_null`, `unique`), and documenting final tables distinguish candidates from 90% of the competition.
3. **Python for advanced analysis and automation:** While SQL excels at manipulating structured data in a data warehouse, Python (with Pandas, NumPy, Matplotlib/Seaborn) is the tool of choice for exploratory analysis, statistics, and reproducible scripting. Loading a CSV into a Pandas DataFrame, performing statistical analysis, and producing clear visualizations is a baseline requirement.

Consider a practical example: a SaaS company wants to calculate growth in Monthly Recurring Revenue (MRR). A junior analyst might write a complex monolithic query. A competent analyst would use SQL and dbt principles to create a robust, reusable data model. Here's part of that logic using CTEs and window functions for clarity and power:

```sql
WITH subscriptions AS (
    -- Source table with subscriptions, one row per customer-month with MRR amount
    SELECT
        customer_id,
        DATE_TRUNC('month', subscription_date)::DATE AS subscription_month,
        mrr
    FROM raw_subscriptions
),
monthly_revenue AS (
    -- Aggregate total MRR per month
    SELECT
        subscription_month,
        SUM(mrr) AS total_mrr
    FROM subscriptions
    GROUP BY 1
),
revenue_growth AS (
    -- Calculate previous month's MRR and percent growth
    SELECT
        subscription_month,
        total_mrr,
        LAG(total_mrr, 1) OVER (ORDER BY subscription_month) AS previous_month_mrr,
        (total_mrr - LAG(total_mrr, 1) OVER (ORDER BY subscription_month)) * 100.0 / LAG(total_mrr, 1) OVER (ORDER BY subscription_month) AS mom_growth_pct
    FROM monthly_revenue
)
SELECT
    subscription_month,
    total_mrr,
    previous_month_mrr,
    ROUND(mom_growth_pct::NUMERIC, 2) AS mom_growth_pct
FROM revenue_growth
ORDER BY subscription_month DESC;
```

This code is not just functional; it is readable, maintainable, and self-documented thanks to CTE usage. It shows understanding beyond syntax, touching software engineering principles applied to data.

## From Metric to Impact: The Netflix Case Study

The most elusive and valuable skill for a data analyst is linking analysis to business decisions that create value. Companies don't pay for charts; they pay for better decisions those charts enable.

Netflix exemplifies a data-driven culture where every significant product change - from poster graphics to recommendation algorithms - is validated through rigorous A/B testing.

Imagine a Netflix Product Manager hypothesizes: "Replacing static previews with auto-playing trailers will increase user engagement." A competent analyst doesn't just "measure results." They actively design the experiment:

1. **Define Metrics:** Primary success metric (e.g., play rate) and guardrail metrics (page load time, session abandonment, long-term churn) to ensure no negative side effects.
2. **Calculate Sample Size and Duration:** Based on current conversion rates and minimum detectable effect (e.g., 2% increase in play rate), determine required users per group and test length for statistical significance (p-value < 0.05).
3. **Analyze Results:** Segment outcomes by device, user tenure, region. Did it work better on mobile or smart TVs? Did new users respond differently than long-term users? Did engagement increase in Brazil but decrease in Japan due to cultural differences?

**Hypothetical Scenario with Numbers:**
- Two-week A/B test on 2 million users (1 million per group).
- Primary metric: play rate 18.5% in treatment vs. 17.2% in control, a 7.5% relative increase, statistically significant (p=0.01).
- Guardrail metrics showed no significant negative impact.
- Segment analysis: effect stronger for users with less than six months subscription (+11% play rate) than older users (+4%).

The analyst's conclusion is not "the test worked" but "auto-playing trailers significantly increased engagement, especially among new users, without harming other experience metrics. Recommend full rollout with ongoing 90-day retention monitoring." This is the leap from data-puller to strategic partner.

## The Data Professional's Lab: Building an End-to-End Project

Theory is necessary, but competence is forged in practice. This section offers a roadmap to build your first high-quality portfolio project through three progressive exercises simulating a real analytic workflow.

**Recommended Dataset:** NYC TLC Trip Record Data, a rich, realistic, and sufficiently "messy" public dataset. Choose one month (e.g., Yellow Taxi Trip Records for January 2023).

### Exercise 1: Exploration and Cleaning with SQL (Basic Level)
- Load Parquet or CSV into a database (DuckDB recommended locally, or PostgreSQL/BigQuery Sandbox).
- Inspect data with SQL queries: total trips, date range, min/max values for `trip_distance`, `fare_amount`, `passenger_count`.
- Identify and document anomalies: zero distance or duration trips, negative amounts. Write a CTE filtering these rows, explaining filters in code comments or README. Save this cleaning query as base for further analysis.

### Exercise 2: Descriptive Analysis and Hypothesis Formulation (Intermediate Level)
- Aggregate metrics: average trip distance and fare by weekday, top 10 pickup-dropoff zone pairs, trip distribution by passenger count.
- Temporal analysis: trips per hour, weekday vs. weekend volumes.
- Formulate 2-3 business hypotheses based on results. Example: "Credit card payments have higher average tip percentages than cash because POS interfaces suggest default tip options."

### Exercise 3: Hypothesis Testing and Visualization (Advanced Level)
- Write complex SQL query testing chosen hypothesis (e.g., average tip percentage by payment type, excluding outliers).
- Export aggregated results to CSV. Use Python (Matplotlib/Seaborn) or BI tools (Tableau Public, Looker Studio) to create clear visual comparisons.
- Document entire work in a GitHub project with README following the recommended structure, including queries, final visualization, and in-depth discussion of results and limitations.

Completing this lab provides not just a CV line but a compelling story to share in interviews, including technical challenges, analytic discoveries, and a tangible final product.

## Conceptual Model

Work competence emerges when three elements converge: **real problem**, **reproducible method**, and **communicable output**. Missing any means you're doing exercises, improvising, or delivering invisible value.

Your portfolio must show not only what you can use but how you think: initial question, available data, cleaning, assumptions, analysis, limits, and proposed decisions.

## Rigorous Formalization

For each project, write a minimal card: supported decision, dataset, metric definitions, main queries, quality checks, final visualization, and recommendation. This card matters more than dashboard aesthetics because it lets readers judge your reasoning's reliability.

A professional project doesn't promise absolute certainty; it also reveals unknowns, missing data, and needed evidence to strengthen conclusions.

## Example or Case Study

The NYC taxi project becomes compelling when it shifts from "I analyzed a public dataset" to "I investigated conditions increasing average tip, controlling for payment method, distance, time, and zone." This distinction matters in interviews: you demonstrate not only technical skill but the ability to turn raw data into business conversations.

## Lab / Exercise

### Basic Level

Choose a public dataset and write a README with business question, relevant columns, metrics, and initial anomalies.

### Intermediate Level

Build reproducible queries or notebooks to clean data, calculate metrics, and document filters.

### Research-Grade Level

Turn the project into a full decision memo: hypothesis, analysis, visualization, main limitation, recommendation, and next analysis steps.

### Recommended Datasets and Materials

Use NYC TLC Trip Record Data, synthetic e-commerce data, public SaaS datasets, or anonymized personal exports. Publish code, README, and a brief decision note in the same repository.

## Common Pitfall to Avoid

The most frequent mistake is building a portfolio as a tool gallery: some SQL, some Python, a dashboard. Recruiters don't want a tool inventory. They want evidence you can isolate a problem, choose a method, and defend a recommendation.

## Summary

The path from student to data professional is an iterative cycle of learning, applying, and demonstrating. The theoretical knowledge gained is fuel, but practical competence, crystallized in a solid, well-documented portfolio, is the engine that leads to job offers. Companies hire problem-solvers, not certificates. Every project you complete, every dataset you explore, and every insight you communicate is a step toward proving you can work with data to create value. Your career will be defined not by the queries you write but by the quality of questions you ask and the decisions your analyses influence.
