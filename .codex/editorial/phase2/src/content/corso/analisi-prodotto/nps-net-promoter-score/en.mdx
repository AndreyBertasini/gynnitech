# NPS and Satisfaction: Net Promoter Score Without Superstition

NPS is often seen as a satisfaction indicator, but reality is more nuanced: some users churn even as the score improves. This lesson explains how to treat NPS as a signal to integrate with other data, not as an absolute truth.

## Real-world problem

In product analytics, understanding where the product creates real value versus apparent activity is crucial. NPS is often treated as a number to improve at all costs, without linking it to concrete decisions. The goal is to use NPS to support operational choices grounded in data, explicit assumptions, and minimal controls.

## Conceptual model

| Phase     | What to clarify                      | Output                 |
|-----------|------------------------------------|------------------------|
| Question  | Which real decision should improve? | Decision to make       |
| Measure   | Which observable signal represents the problem? | Metric or source data  |
| Control   | Which baseline makes the result interpretable? | Credible comparison   |
| Action    | What changes after analysis?        | Next operational step  |

This simple model keeps focus on decision, data, control, and action.

## Rigorous formalization

To analyze NPS properly, define the unit of work (user, cohort, product event, feature, or journey), link it to an observable metric (activation, retention, frequency, conversion, churn, cohort value), and declare the expected decision (product diagnosis, experiment, prioritization, UX intervention).

| Element         | Required specification                              |
|-----------------|----------------------------------------------------|
| Unit of analysis| user, cohort, product event, feature, or journey   |
| Main signal     | activation, retention, frequency, conversion, churn, cohort value |
| Baseline        | previous period, comparable group, benchmark, counterfactual scenario |
| Decision        | product diagnosis, experiment, prioritization, UX intervention |
| Risk            | Mistaking an available number for sufficient proof |

Formalization should allow another analyst to reproduce the logic and reach the same decision.

## Analytical deconstruction of Net Promoter Score: formula, biases, and context

NPS comes from a simple question: "On a scale from 0 to 10, how likely are you to recommend [product] to a friend?" Responses fall into:

- Promoters (9-10): enthusiastic customers driving growth
- Passives (7-8): satisfied but indifferent
- Detractors (0-6): dissatisfied, potentially harmful

Formula:

`NPS = % Promoters - % Detractors`

Score ranges from -100 to +100.

This categorization introduces biases:

- **Selection bias:** who answers the survey? Only loyal customers or those with issues?
- **Timing bias:** when is NPS asked? After success or failure?
- **Cultural bias:** some cultures avoid extremes, skewing distribution.

These factors make interpreting NPS without context risky.

## From vanity to diagnosis: power of segmentation and correlation

An aggregated NPS is a vanity metric. Segment by plan, tenure, feature usage to uncover meaningful differences.

For example, a neobank with +45 overall NPS may hide +78 among experienced "Metal" users and +15 among new "Standard" users. The real issue is improving onboarding for Standard users, not just raising overall NPS.

Correlate NPS with behavior (e.g., churn, referrals). A promoter who doesn't refer and churns like a passive questions predictive validity.

### Case study: Netflix and retention prediction

Netflix uses NPS to predict churn. Logistic regression models show promoters have 10-25% lower churn probability than passives and detractors. This assigns economic value to NPS and guides targeted investments, like improving content or recommendation algorithms based on textual feedback.

## Beyond NPS: satisfaction metrics framework (CSAT and CES)

NPS measures loyalty and word-of-mouth over time. For a full view, other metrics are needed:

- **CSAT (Customer Satisfaction Score):** satisfaction with a single interaction, e.g., delivery or support. Usually 1-5 scale, % positive responses.
- **CES (Customer Effort Score):** perceived effort to complete an action. Reducing effort often predicts loyalty better than delighting customers.

Metric choice depends on the question:

- NPS for loyalty and organic growth
- CSAT for quality of touchpoints
- CES for friction in critical processes

Mature organizations integrate all three in a holistic system.

## Operational loop: turning satisfaction data into action

Data only matters if it drives concrete improvements. The operational loop turns feedback into traceable actions.

### Case study: Stripe and developer feedback analysis

Stripe collects CSAT and comments on API docs. An automated workflow:

1. Negative feedback sent to Slack channel tagging responsible PMs and tech leads
2. Python script analyzes comments to extract recurring themes
3. Creates Jira/GitHub tickets to prioritize fixes

The Python script extracts bigrams from detractor comments to identify common issues like "example code", "missing parameter", or "stripe connect".

This automation reduced negative feedback and improved satisfaction, closing the operational loop.

## Example or case study

Enterprise users may give high NPS but open many tickets, while small teams with medium NPS renew easily. Satisfaction scores must be compared with behavior, segment, and customer value before guiding the roadmap.

| Observed evidence       | Cautious interpretation                  | Recommended action             |
|------------------------|------------------------------------------|-------------------------------|
| Number improves        | Could be real effect or normal variation | Seek comparisons and segmentation |
| One segment changes more | Average hides important differences      | Separate cohorts or use cases    |
| Cost rises with result  | Impact must be read on margin             | Estimate trade-offs and sustainability |

## Common mistake to avoid

Using NPS as a label without process: showing a chart without decision, a metric without baseline, or a conclusion without stating critical assumptions. The key question is: if this result were unstable, what wrong choice would I make? Without a concrete answer, analysis is disconnected from action.
