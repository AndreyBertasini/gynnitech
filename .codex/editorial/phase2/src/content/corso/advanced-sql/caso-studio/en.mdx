# Advanced Lab: professional queries on real-world cases

FashionHub is a fashion retailer with 85 stores in Italy and an e-commerce site. The CMO wants to know which marketing channels generate true incremental revenue, not just attributed revenue. The product team wants to compare the lifetime value of omnichannel customers versus single-channel ones. The CFO demands revenue reconciliation between Shopify and the data warehouse, with a 1.8% discrepancy. The data is raw and requires cleaning, reconciliation, and interpretation to support concrete decisions.

## Real problem

In advanced SQL work, producing numbers is not enough. A disciplined process is needed to turn raw data into reliable decisions by making assumptions, controls, and risks explicit. This lab simulates a real workflow where each step reduces uncertainty and builds credibility.

## Conceptual model

| Step | What to clarify | Output |
|---|---|---|
| Question | Which real choice to improve? | Decision to make |
| Measure | Which observable signal? | Metric or data |
| Control | Which baseline makes it interpretable? | Credible comparison |
| Action | What changes after analysis? | Next operational step |

This simple framework guides building analyses that become decision tools, not just exercises.

## Rigorous formalization

Define the unit of analysis (row, cohort, window), the main signal (correctness, duplicates, stability), the baseline (previous period or benchmark), and the expected decision (query, model, test). Without this formalization, analysis risks ambiguity and irreproducibility.

| Element | Specification |
|---|---|
| Unit of analysis | row, partition, cohort, time metric |
| Signal | correctness, duplicates, stability |
| Baseline | previous period, benchmark |
| Decision | query, model, test |
| Risk | mistaking a number for proof |

## Example or case study

The lab combines deduplication, retention, attribution, segmentation, and reconciliation on real datasets. The goal is not the shortest query but readable, testable, and defensible code. For example, deduplicating transactions keeps only the most recent with status "completed".

| Evidence | Careful reading | Recommended action |
|---|---|---|
| Number improves | Could be normal variation | Seek segments and comparisons |
| Segment changes | Aggregate mean hides differences | Separate cohorts or cases |
| Cost grows | Impact must be read on margin | Estimate trade-offs |

## Lab / exercise

### Basic level

Write a one-page brief describing the decision to support, primary metric, baseline, risk, and action if the signal is confirmed.

### Intermediate level

Build a table with segments or scenarios, indicating what changes, plausible alternative explanations, and controls before recommending action.

### Research-grade level

Prepare a decision memo with hypotheses, data needed, exclusion criteria, quality controls, decision threshold, residual risk, and monitoring plan.

### Recommended datasets and materials

Use real or synthetic data with at least 200 rows, time dimension, segments, and outcome metrics.

## Common mistake to avoid

Do not treat this lab as a label or isolated exercise. The most common error is presenting charts or metrics without clearly linking analysis to a concrete decision, baseline, or risk. The control question is: if this result were unstable, what wrong choice would I make?

## Quiz or checkpoint

1. Which concrete decision does this lesson improve?
2. Which unit of analysis makes the problem measurable?
3. Which baseline avoids naive readings?
4. Which common mistake could change the conclusion?
5. What output would you deliver to a non-technical stakeholder?

## Operational summary

This lab is useful only if it produces clearer decisions, not just terms or numbers. The problem, model, formalization, example, lab, and checkpoint framework turns the lesson into verifiable practice.

## Case study: FashionHub

FashionHub faces three challenges:

- The CMO wants to understand incremental revenue by marketing channel, beyond last-click attribution.
- The product team wants to compare LTV of omnichannel vs single-channel customers.
- The CFO requests revenue reconciliation between Shopify and the data warehouse.

We have four main tables: `customers`, `transactions`, `marketing_touchpoints`, `shopify_settlements`.

### Exercise 1: Cleaning and preparation

Identify duplicate transactions and keep only the most recent with status "completed." Find days without marketing data, indicating tracking issues.

### Exercise 2: Cohort analysis by marketing channel

Assign each customer to the channel of their first touchpoint and calculate retention and cumulative revenue at 1, 3, 6, and 12 months. Compare LTV across channels with confidence intervals.

### Exercise 3: Omnichannel vs single-channel segmentation

Label customers as omnichannel if they have transactions on multiple channels in the last 12 months. Compare key metrics and test statistical significance. Build a migration funnel with a simplified Kaplan-Meier curve.

### Exercise 4: Revenue reconciliation

Compare monthly revenue between data warehouse and Shopify. Segment by day and weekday to diagnose discrepancies. Identify missing transactions with anti-joins.

### Exercise 5: Final recommendation

Summarize insights, operational recommendations with estimated impact, and risks in a table with evidence queries, to deliver to the CMO.

## Delivery

SQL views must be created in the `analytics_fashionhub` schema with clear names and comments, executable in sequence to reconstruct the analytical pipeline.
