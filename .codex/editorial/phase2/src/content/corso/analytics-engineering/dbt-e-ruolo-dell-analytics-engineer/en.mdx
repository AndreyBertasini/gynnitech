# Layering: staging, intermediate, marts

A data warehouse becomes fragile when every query cleans, joins, and interprets data in its own way. The concept of **layering: staging, intermediate, marts** introduces a straightforward discipline: separate normalization, reusable logic, and final data products, so each step has a clear owner and abstraction level.

## Real problem

In analytics engineering, layering solves the problem of turning raw data into tested, documented, and reusable business models. Without it, each team or analyst creates their own version of the same logic, untested and undocumented. This leads to inconsistencies, inefficiencies, and maintenance challenges, especially when metric definitions change.

## Conceptual model

Layering relies on three distinct levels, each with precise responsibilities:

| Layer | Responsibility | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Staging (stg_) | Minimal cleaning and alignment with source data. No business logic. | `stg_stripe__payments` mirrors Stripe's `payments` table with renamed columns and correct types |
| Intermediate (int_) | Complex business logic, joins, intermediate aggregations, and flags. Centralizes logic reused by multiple teams. | `int_orders_with_customers` joins orders and customers, calculates metrics like `net_revenue` |
| Marts (mrt_) | Datasets ready for BI and analysts, denormalized and simple. No complex business logic. | `mrt_marketing__campaign_performance` with aggregated metrics ready for reporting |

## Rigorous formalization

To analyze and apply layering, define the unit of work (source, model, test, mart, metric, exposure) and link it to observable signals like freshness, lineage, test coverage, model cost, and stakeholder trust. The expected decision might be a dbt model, semantic layer, contract, test, or release pipeline.

| Element | Specification |
|---|---|
| Unit of analysis | source, model, test, mart, metric, or exposure |
| Main signal | freshness, lineage, test coverage, model cost, stakeholder trust |
| Baseline | previous period, comparable group, benchmark, counterfactual scenario |
| Decision | dbt model, semantic layer, contract, test, release pipeline |
| Risk | mistaking available data for sufficient proof |

## Example or case study

A European media company with 30 million monthly active users had over 200 dbt models, all marts without layering. This flat structure made dependencies unclear and metric consistency hard to maintain. The analytics engineering team refactored in three phases:

1. Dependency mapping with `dbt docs` and DAG to identify duplicated logic.
2. Extracting duplicated logic into intermediate models and updating marts to reference them.
3. Adding automated tests and CI rules to prevent business logic in marts.

The benefits were clear: faster onboarding, quicker metric updates with fewer errors, and greater transparency for finance and marketing teams.

## Lab / exercise

### Basic level

Write a one-page summary of layering: the decision it supports, primary metric, baseline for comparison, main risk, and the action if the signal is confirmed.

### Intermediate level

Build a table with three segments or periods. For each, indicate what changes, a plausible alternative explanation, and which control you would apply before recommending action.

### Research-grade level

Prepare a detailed decision memo: hypotheses, required data, exclusion criteria, quality controls, decision threshold, residual risk, and post-decision monitoring plan.

### Recommended datasets and materials

Use dbt, a data warehouse, CRM sources, events, marts, semantic layer, and lineage. If real data is unavailable, create a synthetic dataset with at least 200 rows, a time dimension, a segment dimension, and an outcome metric.

## Common mistake to avoid

The most frequent error is treating layering as a label rather than a process. This happens when a team presents a chart without a clear decision, a metric without a baseline, or a conclusion without stating which assumptions could invalidate it. The key question is: if this result were unstable, what wrong choice would I make? Without a concrete answer, the link between analysis and action is missing.

## Quiz or checkpoint

2. Which unit of analysis makes the problem measurable?
3. What baseline would you use to avoid naive interpretation?
4. Which common mistake could change the conclusion?
5. What output would you deliver to a non-technical stakeholder?

Layering becomes truly useful when it leads to clearer decisions and concrete actions, not just terminology. Applying the problem, model, formalization, example, lab, and checkpoint framework turns the lesson into verifiable applied skill.

## Segment interpretation

Don't stop at the aggregate mean. Segment by channel, cohort, plan, country, device, and user maturity. If two segments move in opposite directions, the average can be misleading and lead to wrong decisions.

## Operational decision

Every analysis must end with a choice: continue, stop, iterate, invest, remove, or deepen. If layering does not change the decision, the link between metric and action is still missing.

## Quality control

Before applying layering, check completeness, duplicates, time zones, changed definitions, and excluded segments. Many analyses fail because the starting data measures a different behavior than the team believes.

## Why three layers (not two or four)

The three-layer pattern emerged by community convergence around 2020, not dogma but empirical effectiveness. Each layer has a precise responsibility and does not invade others.

### Staging

Reads raw data, renames columns, casts types, filters technically invalid rows. No joins or business logic.

### Intermediate

Contains complex business logic, joins, intermediate aggregations, and flags. Centralizes logic reused by multiple teams.

### Marts

Datasets ready for consumption, denormalized and simple. No complex logic.

## Naming conventions

The dbt community recommends:

```
stg_[source]__[table_name]     → stg_stripe__payments
int_[description]               → int_daily_customer_revenue
mrt_[domain]__[description]     → mrt_marketing__campaign_performance
```

Always use clear names without cryptic abbreviations, plural for entity tables.

## Concrete example: Monthly Active User (MAU) definition

Without layering, each team writes its own query with different definitions. With layering, cleaning is in staging, business logic in intermediate, and the final metric in marts, ensuring consistency and reusability.

```sql
-- Staging: stg_app__events
-- Intermediate: int_user_activity_flags
SELECT user_id, event_date,
  CASE WHEN event_date > CURRENT_DATE - 30 THEN 1 ELSE 0 END AS is_active_30d
-- Marts: mrt_metrics__mau
SELECT DATE_TRUNC('month', event_date) AS month,
  COUNT(DISTINCT CASE WHEN is_active_30d = 1 THEN user_id END) AS mau
```
